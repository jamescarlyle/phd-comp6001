\begin{abstract}
    This impact assessment applies the ORBIT-RRI AREA (Anticipate, Reflect, Engage, Act) framework to evaluate TriboAI's proposed technology for non-destructive, non-interruptive monitoring of bearing conditions in machinery. The technology aims to reduce unnecessary and premature bearing replacement whilst preventing catastrophic failures. The aim is to ensure innovation is socially desirable, ethically sound, and responsibly developed.

    \keywords{tribology \and bearing \and machine learning \and sensor \and maintenance.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Context}
    Preventative maintenance is key to ensure the ongoing integrity of critical-safety mechanical parts such as bearings. Bearings are subject to various wear mechanisms, and in time their lubricants gradually break down. Eventually this causes the bearing surfaces to rub, accelerating wear and leading to catastrophic failure. However, preventative early replacement is conservative, expensive, and damages the environment. 
    
    Separately, new plant-derived 'green' lubricants are proposed, but lack of trust in their performance will limit adoption. 
    
    The aim of this project is to develop academic research of in-bearing sensors, combine it with machine learning (ML), and commercialise it through a university spin-out called \enquote{TriboAI}. The project has published a timeline which defines its Proof of Concept (PoC), route to market, and sales timeline, with the goal to transition to TriboAI within two years of the conclusion of the PoC. 
    
    This report is loosely based on the AREA 4P Framework, which combines four dimensions (Purpose, Product, People, Process) with four stages (Anticipate, Reflect, Engage, Act). The goal is to ensure that all responsible innovation aspects are addressed.
\section{Beneficial Impacts}
    The key benefits of providing better current-state and remaining-life prediction are reduced disruption, and lower financial and environmental cost. Because many sustainable technologies such as offshore wind and wave power use hard-to-monitor bearings, and because lower operational costs increase adoption, this research can also indirectly support decarbonisation around the world. Two additional benefits are anticipated; self-powered sensors can be used where external power supplies are not allowed in highly-regulated industries, and sensors can also detect external environmental issues (e.g.\ bearing sensors can detect rail track failure).
    
    Elsewhere, the project offers possible socio-economic benefits such as reduced operating costs, extended asset life, lower emissions and fuel use, and better asset availability. It offers broad applications across sectors, including travel, manufacturing and logistics, and addresses several of the UN's Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) without falling foul of others.
\section{Purpose}
\subsection{Anticipation - whether this work should be done at all}
    The work is significant in its potential to reduce the amount of waste associated with too-early, precautionary replacement of bearings. There are many examples where bearing maintenance is avoided in a desire to reduce maintenance costs. Bearing failures in trains can result in catastrophic accidents, and in one example in Ohio, a defective wheel bearing caused a derailment and fire of 38 freight trucks with hazardous materials \cite{wikipedia_2023}.  
    
    The project's purpose aligns with societal needs for safer transportation, reduced environmental waste, and more efficient maintenance operations. Research indicates that predictive maintenance can deliver 18-25\% cost reductions vs.\ a standard approach, and up to 40\% savings over reactive approaches\cite{content_2025}. Additionally, more accurate monitoring will allow more bearings to be remodelled - a refurbished bearing can reach 90\% of the lifespan of a new bearing with just 10\% of the energy and material costs, but only if it has been caught in time\cite{peverill_2021}.
    
    So the main benefit of the technology is that catastrophic failure can be avoided, while at the same time, over-eager and unnecessary early replacement can be deferred. These are socio-economic benefits, but additionally, emissions and fuel consumption can be reduced, utilisation can be increased, and more broadly, industry can be helped to move towards net-zero and decarbonisation. 
\subsection{Beneficiaries}
    The immediate beneficiaries of this technology are the machinery operators. Although the project requires deployment of new bearings fitted with the necessary sensors, and new analysis technology to \enquote{listen} for early perturbations, the ongoing operational costs are lower, via fewer replacements and fewer catastrophic failures. Ultimate users, such as train passengers, will also face fewer disruptions to the services they receive. The difference will be felt through a reduction in ongoing maintenance costs (e.g.\ destructive testing), as well as an decrease in unplanned outage and maintenance.

    Success can directly be measured by the increased utilisation of bearings (i.e.\ they are run until they are almost worn out, but not beyond), a reduction in the number of unnecessary maintenance windows, and a reduction in the number of catastrophic failures. Indirectly, success can be measured by service availability, reputation and end-user satisfaction.
\subsection{How sustainable is the product/outcome?}
    The \emph{project research} just requires sustainable, small-scale tests using a few thousand bearings. In \emph{production}, considering train bearings alone, the market was valued at roughly USD 1–3.5 billion in the mid-2020s, perhaps \~10 million bearings. Perhaps 1 million of these are replaced and discarded unnecessarily each year, meaning that the material benefits vastly outweigh the costs. This comparison doesn't even account for the huge field of industrial bearing deployment; including aircraft, ships, and most factory machinery. 
    
    The additional sensor material (for example, capacitively coupled electrodes) itself amounts to a few grams per bearing and is not significant from an environmental waste perspective. The \emph{product} overall is positively sustainable since each bearing will be more efficiently utilised. Although manufacture cost of sensor-enabled bearings will be higher, the incremental unit cost will fall over time and as quantities increase, so that the technology can become commoditised after any patent protection has ended. No significant material is used in manufacture of the sensors of bearings.
    
    Still, the materials used for manufacture of sensors should be assessed for environmental impact - care should be taken that the recycling of steel remains feasible, as separation of materials during recycling may not be possible. During operation, the active lifespan of bearings should be logged and compared against a control statistic of bearings without sensors.
     
    Overall, the system is focused on long-term benefits, because in the short term, the amortisation cost of bearing development, initial installation, and monitoring infrastructure, as well as staff training, will be higher. But improvements should be visible from the moment the enhanced bearings are monitored.

\subsection{Technology readiness}
    The project considers itself to be at Technology Readiness Level 6, \enquote{demonstrator} level. Several key hurdles exist: The industries targeted are highly regulated in many cases, so safety concerns take the highest priority. And for a move away from polluting oil-based lubricants to plant-based ones, it is hard to overcome the natural conservatism that machinery operators demonstrate from their years of experience with conventional lubricants.
\subsection{Potential of misuse}
    The applications of the technology are extremely wide-ranging and can be applied to most industries. Examples provided by the project include wind turbines, trains, trucks, aircraft, gas turbines and all power generation plant. In all of these cases, inspection requires disassembly and is dangerous and costly. Given the wide range of applicability, consideration must be given to military application. But the technology is not aimed at the military sector per se., and in any case, classifying military use as \emph{misuse} is a matter of perspective.

    The technology doesn't directly facilitate illegal behaviour.
\subsection{Consequences of failure}
    If the project fails to deliver a working PoC (i.e.\ the bearings, their sensors, and the peripheral ML-based analysis tools), the negative consequences are limited; financial (to the extent that funding doesn't deliver a return) and possibly reputational (to the extent that e.g.\ exaggerated claims were not met). 
    
    However, once the bearings are deployed into production, the negative consequences are much greater: If the sensors produce a false positive, then machinery will be taken out of service and components replaced unnecessarily. If they produce a false negative, and don't detect an imminent failure, then life-threatening accidents might occur. 
    
    The act of all engineering is to balance risk, safety margin and cost; this technology allows operators to understand failure boundaries better, and to safely reduce uncertainty and cost.
\subsection{Knowledge sharing}
    A key decision for the project will be the extent to which to share research results and learnings which might be transferable to other fields. 
    
    The team has experience of publishing papers and software openly, for example in the form of software libraries on Github (e.g.\ github.com/jogrundy), and has published 800 research papers collectively. It has a track record of organising conferences and symposia, chairing industry bodies, and outreach and teaching activities.
    
    The team has already published less sensitive results, but recognises that the core innovation, electrostatic sensing for early stage wear detection, is a novel approach that can be protected through patents. 
\subsection{Intellectual property} 
    Patent searches have not identified specific prior art. The team has presented to the university IP panel, and will seek to protect disclosure of the AM/ML algorithms and the weights etc.\ evaluated. Following patent attorney advice, the team will decide on whether simply to retain trade secrets or disclose through the protection of a patent application. Electrostatic sensors themselves are not novel; the team published a paper describing the application in bearing wear detection in 2021. 
    
    Given this position, the team is limiting wider communication. Conference and journal publications are being held back, and partner discussions are held under NDA to limit disclosure. 

    Other aspects of IP are also being developed, such as protection by copyright of the software solution and printed sensor design. The objective is to allow the university to license the IP portfolio to TriboAI Ltd., which will apply for trademark protection across related classes of goods and services. It is the intention that the IP will represent a significant company asset.

    The spin-out team will also maintain an IP register and follow a formal process of IP due diligence, looking at identification, ownership and barriers for IP components (some of which will be held by other parties).

    This stance on IP protection through non-disclosure is standard within the commercial startup world. Patents and trade secrets can increase financial returns from research, encouraging firms to invest in new green and socially beneficial technologies, such as this. However, overly broad patents can block following research, and restrict the open exploration that RRI encourages. And if IP protection is sought solely to maximise profit, it will conflict with responsible goals of equitable access and long term societal benefit. The team should weigh the needs for protection against long-term sustainability, and consider a tiered licensing model (with robust protection) where certain social-benefit sectors can gain access.
\subsection{Product alternatives}
    Alternative approaches of preventative failure detection exist, but they are disruptive (i.e.\ detection requires the machinery to be suspended from service), involved (i.e.\ they require disassembly for inspection) and potentially are destructive in nature (e.g.\ a sample of bearings are sliced open to inspect sliding surfaces). 

    The other alternative is the status quo, but as discussed elsewhere, this involves unsustainable practice of the conservative early retirement of bearings, and creates waste.
\section{People}
    The project is led by Professor Robert Wood and Professor Nick Harris, with a founding team from the University of Southampton. Support is also resourced from the university, both in terms of tribological laboratory facilities as well as business support. The technology transfer officer is Graham Cradock, who has been supporting spin-outs from the university for 3 years and has many prior years of CEO experience at startups.

    Each member of the project team has extensive delivery experience. For example, Professor Wood has 30 years experience on developing electrostatic sensors, and has been awarded medals in the tribology domain, as well as funding to create the UK national centre for tribology. Other members of the founding team have 25+ years experience in tribology, and have experience spinning out cutting-edge technology into startups such as Perpetuum, which grew to 75 people and was sold to Hitachi. 
\subsection{Impact on people}
    If the research is successful, and the spin-out achieves commercial traction and adoption, machine operators should see shorter and fewer machinery maintenance windows, while service end-users should see higher service availability and overall lower costs.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Machinery Operators}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reduced maintenance costs, as the amount of maintenance work will fall.
        \item Improved asset utilisation, safety performance.
        \item Extended equipment life.
        \item Reduced unplanned downtime.
        \item Longer-term cost savings.
        \item Engineering jobs will need to adapt to an increase in sophistication.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Maintenance Workers}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Replacing bearings on heavy machinery can be dangerous and dirty, so job quality improves.
        \item Job evolves from reactive repair work to predictive monitoring.
        \item Upskilling, with additional training needed to interpret sensor monitoring data.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{End-users}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Improved safety.
        \item Increased service reliability through fewer unexpected failures.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bearing Manufacturers}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Potential market shift from replacement sales to re-manufacturing services.
        \item Growth in monitoring and digital service offerings.
        \item Loss of market for bearing manufacturers who rely solely on volume.
        \item Proposed bearings are more sophisticated, and greater added value.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item Need for new industrial standards.
        \item Development of updated compliance frameworks.

    The project founders predict that the project and commercialisation will generate over 20 skilled jobs within 3 years, and contribute to the growth in the UK's clean-tech sector. This could be a huge underestimate if adoption is successful. There are many opportunities to grow; the world's largest bearing manufacturer, SKF, employs around 40,000 people globally.
\subsection{Accountability}
    The research project is managed by the Faculty of Engineering in the University of Southampton, and as long as activity sits under the university's umbrella, the faculty leadership is accountable. After spin-out, the CEO of the spin-out will become accountable to shareholders and wider society. For operating companies adopting the product, accountability ultimately will fall to the CEOs of those organisation.
\subsection{Inclusion}
    The project team has focused on the inclusion of experts, with each member bringing significant prior experience in their area, whether that is organisational and commercial leadership, hardware technology, ML algorithms, or product management. The project is inclusive in the sense that it understands the stakeholders involved (research scientists, industry bodies, partners and customers) and has engaged with them ahead of funding and product development.
\subsection{Diversity}
    The team is diverse in terms of age, experience, skill-set and gender. The focus, though, is on expertise and ability rather than externally defined spectra such as race or social background.
    The project team is currently small (6 founding team members), with a partner network, a university support team, and an advisory board of industrial advisors, bearing and data experts. It is relatively easy to ensure such a small team remains cohesive and inclusive. Of course, inclusion of all members in a relatively small team relies on effective and open communication, and on the ability of the leadership of the project and subsequent spin-out to listen.
\subsection{Upskilling and training}
    Additional training will be required in the organisations which deploy this technology. Although the product will produce human-understandable insights and actions for pre-emptive maintenance alongside raw data, there will still be a new set of operational and analytical skills required from machinery maintenance workers. The project should consider skills transfer to customers. The product will require clear and effective documentation, probably web-based and available in multiple languages. The spin-out may consider some additional consultancy services to transfer knowledge into the customers' teams, but this cannot be at the expense of a lack of focus on continued product development.
    
    Within the current project team, there is a good blend of managerial and technical skill and experience, and no perceived gaps. The team is very lean; every member has a defined area of responsibility. The structure and titles of team members in the organisation indicates a recognition of expertise in specific domains. That said, the founding team are all primarily technologists, supported by external commercial expertise from the advisory board and the university support team (business development advisor, etc.). The spin-out will want to strengthen internal commercial expertise  to support sales expansion.
\section{Processes}
\subsection{Governance}
    The project has an experienced advisory board. The board consists of a data scientist, an industry adviser and a bearing expert. Given this expertise, it's expected that the board would focus primarily on technical matters and direction, rather than commercial, leadership or team issues.

    In terms of technology development, the project promotes non-destructive sensing methods that are well established. The international standards which map closest to the project are the ISO standards for machinery vibration, such as ISO 10816 and its current replacement ISO/DIS 20816-3 \cite{for_2025}. The research methodology should incorporate laboratory testing, with controlled experiments to validate sensor accuracy and algorithm performance across bearing types and operating conditions. This should be followed by field trials, real-world deployment on operating machinery to assess performance under variable environmental conditions. 
\subsection{Engagement and stakeholder representation}
    The experienced team understands the importance of stakeholder inclusion. Stakeholders are managed by the executive project team, with oversight provided by the board. Over the last year, the founders have engaged with commercial stakeholders to verify industrial needs in condition monitoring. As part of an Innovate UK ICURe Explore program, the founders have conducted targeted market research, including requirements and use-case gathering. To support continuous improvement of the Minimum Viable Product (MVP), the project team will engage with partners in solution testing, joint discussions, and iterative feedback. The team will also build an expert community through partners where insight and lessons can be shared. In addition, the team will undertake direct outreach, including site visits, trade show participation, partner introduction and online webinars. This outreach activity is ongoing, with an open online survey, which allows potential customers to verify requirements. This helps shape the product roadmap, define the development plan and shape the MVP.
\subsection{Data}
    Data associated with the project will be handled under an overall Data Management Plan ensuring that all data are FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Specifically for tribological data, the project will adopt commonly agreed standards for the generation of data and metadata \cite{garabedian_2022}. Data should be stored in open, non-proprietary formats (like CSV) so it can be easily found, reused, and combined with other data. All data management must follow GDPR and UK regulation. Commercial partners' data must be kept private while still providing enough access to meet the project's analytical requirements.
    
    Statistical data, such as the number of failed bearings or catastrophic failures, should also be recorded, as this may assist in product safety/recall situations. The amount of time that machinery spends 'in maintenance' where bearings are being replaced should also be measured.
\subsection{Risks and Risk Management}
    The proposed product has several risks, including long-term sensor reliability in harsh operating environments, algorithm performance across diverse bearing types and operating conditions, and market acceptance and regulatory approval timelines. A non-exhaustive list of risks includes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Risk:} Technical - e.g.\ vibrations detected by sensors cannot be correlated to the level of wear and bearing life remaining, or sensor calibration drift, environmental interference, and data transmission failures could lead to unreliable predictions.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Effect:} Failure to gain credibility with manufacturers and operators.
            \item \textbf{Mitigation:} Prior technology research, prior experience in overcoming go-to-market scale-up issues.
        \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Risk:} Security - e.g.\ connected monitoring systems can introduce new cybersecurity vulnerabilities.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Effect:} Service disruption as cyber vulnerabilities exploited.
            \item \textbf{Mitigation:} Ongoing cyber penetration testing.
        \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Risk:} Governance controls fail; research and spin-out activity is conducted inappropriately.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Effect:} Inappropriate results generated, ineffective work undertaken.
            \item \textbf{Mitigation:} Oversight from external board, possibly additional focus committees as scale dictates.
        \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Risk:} Commercial - e.g.\ that funding for product development cannot be found, or that the cost of product development and manufacture is so high that it's cheaper to replace bearings early and discard usable items, or dependence on proprietary monitoring systems may create vendor lock-in for operators.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Effect:} Lack of product development and sales.
            \item \textbf{Mitigation:} Commercial partnerships and ongoing validation of commercial feasibility.
            \item \textbf{Mitigation:} 3 partners, increasing the likelihood of successful integration with at least one, but also explore other systems with new partners.
        \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Risk:} Lack of engagement from prospective customers or markets.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Effect:} Validation issues of commercial use cases or attracting investment.
            \item \textbf{Mitigation:} Ongoing customer discovery during ICURe Explore and Future Worlds programmes, and development of an online presence. Contacts with 100+ partners at nCATS and 50 during ICURe Explore.
        \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Risk:} Business opportunity remains at the test level.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Effect:} Difficulty in expanding the business and scale up.
            \item \textbf{Mitigation:} Identified a beachhead market but can move to tribology services and provide connections to larger industry players.
        \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Risk:} Reputational - e.g.\ that the bearing analysis works in a laboratory environment but fails in operational use, causing inconvenience or death to end-users and negative public perception. These reputational risks could impact machine operating organisations, the spin-out, and the university itself. 
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Effect:} Damage to commercial activities, negative public perception, expensive litigation.
            \item \textbf{Mitigation:} Honesty with customers. Effective governance and oversight. Sufficient testing before commercial at-scale deployment
        \end{itemize} 
    \end{itemize}
    Risks can be monitored through the establishment of a risk management process. This ensures that risks are logged in a register and periodically and methodically reassessed. All high-likelihood or high-severity risks should be raised to the advisory board. This is a standard commercial project management process, often part of RAIDs analysis (tracking of Risks, Assumptions, Issues and Dependencies). Further formalisation could be effected through the establishment of a risk committee within the spin-out, and communication of top risks to the university risk process. 
    
    Risks are in part mitigated by the tremendous experience of the team behind the research and spin-out, but additional specific risk analysis and management may be required.
\subsection{Reflection and introspection}
    Reflection is crucial to ensure that blind spots and weaknesses are recognised, so that they may be ameliorated. It's natural for spin-outs and startups to focus hard on what their participants are most excited about, missing the bigger picture of implications and wider environmental issues with ultimately cause them to fail. The project team understands the context in which it operates; through an extensive network of technologists and potential customers, it understands the status quo, the potential to improve bearing monitoring, and the economic benefits of doing so. Reflection is not being monitored as an activity per se, but the founding team has the experience to know that reflection (whether formalised, externalised or internalised) is important. 
    
    Additionally, the advisory board should hold a mirror up to the founding team, and challenge team members to continually assess progress, whether original objectives remain valid, whether funding remains sufficient, and whether commercialisation (the ground truth for a spin-out) is on track. As a commercial transition begins, it may be beneficial to add non-executive directors with legal, marketing, or finance experience. 

    The project should ensure that management time is set aside to reflect as a group, for example, on a monthly or annual basis.        
\subsection{Engagement}
    The team's objective is to engage with machine manufacturers, parts suppliers, research institutions and users, and reflects the team's experience in working inclusively with industrial partners and researchers. Customer feedback is seen as critical for impact and market entry and stakeholders already have substantial involvement in the project. External organisations, such as Catapult (the national renewable energy centre), Cummins (a renowned diesel engine manufacture), Schaeffler (a car parts supplier with 80,000 employees), and City University have provided written support for funding applications. These industry and academic partners can provide commercial and technical feedback.
\subsection{Conduct}
    The founding team commits to ethical conduct, open and trusted research, and to working with colleagues without prejudice or bias using inclusive approaches to product design and activities. Team members are focused on effective commercialisation as well as research, so there is a commercial imperative for continued improvement, and the motivation of the team members is strongly aligned with this. As senior members of the university faculty, the founding team already understands the obligation for ethical and unbiased research.
